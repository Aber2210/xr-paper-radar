3.1 System Architecture
The experimental environment was developed in Unity 6 (Editor v6000.2.9f1) and deployed on the Meta Quest 2 headset via Oculus Air Link for high-fidelity PC-VR rendering (Figure  1 ). The virtual character, “Alex” was created using a ReadyPlayerMe avatar. We gave him full-body movements (IK) and idle and speaking animations to make him look and feel more life-like. The setting was a casual cafe, chosen to make the interaction feel like a real-world encounter. In this scenario, Alex was participant’s childhood friend who studies at UC Berkeley but has traveled to Mohali, India to visit the participant. The goal as the participant was to talk to Alex and learn a few specific facts about his life.
We utilized the Google Gemini 2.5 Flash API for intelligence. User speech was transcribed via Google Cloud STT, processed by the LLM (with regex cleaning to remove markdown/emojis), and synthesized via Google Cloud TTS. Average system round-trip latency was approximately 2.0 seconds ( S ​ D = 0.4 ​ s SD=0.4s ). To enhance realism and enforce reliance on verbal cues, no visual transcriptions of the dialogue were displayed to the user.
3.2 Participants
We recruited 18 participants ( M a ​ g ​ e = 21.28 M_{age}=21.28 years, S ​ D = 2.42 SD=2.42 , range: 19–30 years; 9 male, 9 female) following two pilot sessions conducted to refine the experimental procedure. The majority of participants (61.1%, n = 11 n=11 ) had no prior VR experience (first or second time using VR), while 27.8% ( n = 5 n=5 ) had tried VR a handful of times, and 11.1% ( n = 2 n=2 ) used VR occasionally (once every few months). All participants provided informed consent before beginning the study.
3.3 Ethical Considerations
This study was conducted in accordance with ethical research principles. All participants provided informed consent via digital waiver forms prior to participation. Participants were informed that their involvement would require wearing a VR headset and interacting with a 3D avatar in a virtual environment, engaging in conversations across multiple experimental conditions, completing questionnaires and surveys, and participating in a brief debriefing session. The total time commitment was approximately 30 minutes in a single session.
Data collection included automatically tracked data (response times and session logs with timestamps and conversation logs) and self-reported data (demographic information including age, gender, and VR experience level, as well as questionnaire responses regarding perceived latency, immersion, and user experience).
To protect participant privacy, personal information was kept separate from research data. All data were stored securely on an encrypted, password-protected database with access limited to the research team.

4.1 Latency Consistency Verification
To verify that network-dependent LLM latency fluctuations did not systematically bias our feedback condition comparisons, we analyzed the actual response times logged for each condition. The mean response latencies were: No Feedback ( M = 1.94 ​ s M=1.94s , S ​ D = 0.58 ​ s SD=0.58s ), Gestures ( M = 1.98 ​ s M=1.98s , S ​ D = 0.62 ​ s SD=0.62s ), Visual ( M = 2.02 ​ s M=2.02s , S ​ D = 0.51 ​ s SD=0.51s ), and Verbal ( M = 1.92 ​ s M=1.92s , S ​ D = 0.60 ​ s SD=0.60s ).
