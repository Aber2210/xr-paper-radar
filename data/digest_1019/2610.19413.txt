IV-C Dual-mode Control Validation
The dual-mode controller was evaluated through an equal-distance
traversal test using command-dispatch records from a representative
session. The test examined the stepwise output characteristics of
the velocity gears and position mode. Gear 1, Gear 3, Gear 5, and
position mode each moved the guidewire over the same prescribed
distance, and their activation and dispatch intervals were analyzed.
Fig.  5 shows distinct dispatch patterns
and traversal durations for equal-distance movements. Lower gears were
used early in the session, followed by higher gears and position
increments, demonstrating mode changes within one workflow.
Fig. 5: Command dispatch for equal-distance guidewire movements using
Gears 1, 3, and 5 and Position Mode.
The record provides a functional demonstration that the different gear
settings and position mode can be used to complete equal-distance
guidewire movements within one training session.
IV-D Curvature-Based Interaction Response Evaluation
A hybrid simulation scene containing a straight glass tube and an
anatomical vascular model was used to examine the response of the
curvature-derived interaction component. The glass tube provided a
low-bending reference segment, while the vascular model introduced curved
paths that constrained guidewire deformation. This configuration allowed
the guidewire to pass from a nearly straight configuration into
progressively bent configurations within the same navigation sequence.
To examine the repeatability of the curvature-derived interaction
response, the navigation experiment was repeated 20 times under the
same simulation settings. Each recorded trajectory was divided into
three representative stages: straight-tube insertion, entry into the
curved vascular segment, and navigation within the deeper curved segment.
For each trial and stage, the curvature-derived response
F curv , k F_{\mathrm{curv},k} was averaged over all simulation samples within
that stage

In the preliminary study with 15 novice participants, physical-phantom
task success increased from 53.3% before VR training to 93.3% after
training, accompanied by a reduction in mean completion time. More
importantly, the improved post-training performance in the physical
robotic task suggests that the proposed system can support the transfer
of navigation and manipulation skills practiced in VR to a corresponding
physical intervention environment. The interaction feedback and
trajectory-based review may further help trainees recognize potentially
unsafe manipulation patterns rather than focusing solely on task
completion.
These findings provide preliminary support for the proposed framework
as a training and performance-review tool. Because the participant used a single-group pre–post design, however, the observed
improvements cannot be attributed exclusively to VR training. Future
work will validate the interaction estimates against measured forces
and evaluate training effectiveness through randomized controlled
studies with independent training and assessment tasks.
