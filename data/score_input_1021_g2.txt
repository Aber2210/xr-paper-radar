Figure 2: HumanSkill-4D overview. (Offline) We build a semantic memory for each avatar, a motion memory for its driving pose sequence, and an operator registry shared across avatars. (Online) The frozen MLLM selects target categories from their names and descriptions, and their stored scores determine the spatial mask. For a 4D query, it also reads the registry’s text cards and composes operators into a motion program, and running the program on the joint trajectories gives the temporal mask. The two masks give the spatiotemporal selection. In this example, the program identifies intervals when both upper arms move outward, and StateAtEnd retains each movement interval whose last frame has both arms horizontal (orange band).
Given a language query, HumanSkill-4D selects the described region of an avatar and, for a 4D query, every matching interval in its driving pose sequence. As shown in Fig.  2 , the framework prepares reusable memories offline, then uses a frozen MLLM Φ MLLM \Phi_{\mathrm{MLLM}} to interpret queries online.
In the offline phase, we build a semantic memory ℳ semantic \mathcal{M}_{\text{semantic}} that links body-part and garment categories to the avatar’s Gaussians through category scores p i ​ c p_{ic} registered from a frozen 2D human parser Φ parse \Phi_{\mathrm{parse}} . The memory also stores category-level appearance descriptions generated by Φ MLLM \Phi_{\mathrm{MLLM}} from multi-view images (Sec.  4.2 ). A motion memory ℳ motion \mathcal{M}_{\text{motion}} stores joint and root trajectories for each driving pose sequence. A registry ℛ \mathcal{R} shared across avatars provides reusable templates for checking pose and motion conditions, along with temporal relations that specify how these conditions overlap or follow one another in time (Sec.  4.3 ).
In the online phase, Φ MLLM \Phi_{\mathrm{MLLM}} reads the query q q and the category names and descriptions to select target categories 𝒞 q \mathcal{C}_{q} . Their stored scores determine the spatial mask 𝐬 q \mathbf{s}_{q} ( Where ), which is the complete output for a 3D query. For a 4D query, the model also reads the registry’s text cards and composes operators into a motion program Π q \Pi_{q} . A deterministic executor runs this program on ℳ motion \mathcal{M}_{\text{motion}} to obtain the temporal mask 𝐡 q \mathbf{h}_{q} ( When ). The two masks give the spatiotemporal selection 𝐘 q \mathbf{Y}_{q} (Sec.  4.4 ).

Benchmarks. To evaluate avatar referring segmentation, we introduce three benchmarks by augmenting existing human datasets with referring queries and human-annotated targets, following prior evaluations  ( He et al., 2025 ; Li et al., 2025 ) . ZJU-Refer-3D extends ZJU-MoCap  ( Peng et al., 2021 ) with dense part annotations and 20 referring queries covering part names, appearance, wearing relations, and functions. HuMMan-Refer-4D extends HuMMan  ( Cai et al., 2022 ) by converting its fine-grained body-part motion descriptions  ( Zhang et al., 2023 ) into 20 referring queries, while ZJU-Refer-4D extends ZJU-MoCap with 20 queries in the same format. Construction and annotation details appear in Supp. Secs.  B.1 – B.2 .
Metrics. For part segmentation, we report mean per-category IoU (mIoU), overall pixel accuracy (OA), and mean per-category recall (mAcc)  ( Suzuki et al., 2025a ) . For 3D referring segmentation, we report mIoU over queries  ( He et al., 2025 ) . For 4D referring segmentation, vIoU evaluates region accuracy and timing jointly, penalizing both incorrect masks and extra or missed active frames  ( Li et al., 2025 ) . We additionally report tIoU, precision, and recall to assess temporal selection separately  ( Fiebelman et al., 2025 ) . tIoU measures overlap between predicted and annotated active frames. Precision is the fraction of selected frames annotated as active, and recall the fraction of annotated active frames selected (Supp. Sec.  C.1 ).

2.3. Training Objective and Optimization
The overall training objective combines rendering loss, geometric objective, and camera pose regularization. We optimize training under sparse views by combining early stopping and motion-aware dropout to stabilize convergence and improve efficiency.
Rendering Loss
Let 𝐈 ^ \hat{\mathbf{I}} be the rendered image and 𝐈 gt \mathbf{I}_{\text{gt}} the ground truth. We adopt a standard combination of ℓ 1 \ell_{1} and SSIM:
(6)
ℒ render = λ 1 ​ ‖ 𝐈 ^ − 𝐈 gt ‖ 1 + λ SSIM ​ ( 1 − SSIM ​ ( 𝐈 ^ , 𝐈 gt ) ) . \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{render}}=\lambda_{1}\,\|\hat{\mathbf{I}}-\mathbf{I}_{\text{gt}}\|_{1}+\lambda_{\text{SSIM}}\,\big(1-\text{SSIM}(\hat{\mathbf{I}},\mathbf{I}_{\text{gt}})\big).
Geometric Objective
The geometric objective ℒ geo \mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}} combines photometric consistency with depth and flow regularization, as detailed in Section  2.1.1 .
Camera Regularization
The camera regularization term ℒ cam \mathcal{L}_{\text{cam}} constrains the deviation of optimized camera poses from their initial values, preventing excessive pose modifications.
Overall Objective
The complete training objective is a weighted sum of all three components:
(7)
ℒ total = λ render ​ ℒ render + λ geo ​ ℒ geo + λ cam ​ ℒ cam , \displaystyle\mathcal{L}_{\text{total}}=\lambda_{\text{render}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{render}}+\lambda_{\text{geo}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{geo}}+\lambda_{\text{cam}}\mathcal{L}_{\text{cam}},
where λ render \lambda_{\text{render}} , λ geo \lambda_{\text{geo}} , and λ cam \lambda_{\text{cam}} are the weights for the rendering loss, geometric objective, and camera regularization, respectively.

2. Methodology
Our overall algorithm is illustrated in Figure 2 .
Following the deep learning perspective outlined in the introduction, we organize our approach into two complementary categories: Supervision Enhancement and Training Regularization . This unified framework addresses the three fundamental causes of overfitting in sparse-view 3DGS: noisy data (corresponding to camera miscalibration), insufficient data, and excessive model capacity. In the following subsections, we detail each component.
2.1. Supervision Enhancement
To address insufficient supervision and noisy data in sparse-view 3DGS, we enhance the training data through two strategies: pseudo-view synthesis addresses insufficient data, while joint camera–scene optimization addresses noisy data.
2.1.1. Optical Flow Guided Pseudo-view Synthesis
Given a predicted depth map D D with intrinsics ( f x , f y , c x , c y ) (f_{x},f_{y},c_{x},c_{y}) , we synthesize a nearby view by applying a small in-plane translation 𝒕 = ( t x , t y , 0 ) ⊤ \boldsymbol{t}=(t_{x},t_{y},0)^{\top} . The induced pixel shifts are
(1)
u ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) = f x ​ ( − t x ) D ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) , v ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) = f y ​ ( − t y ) D ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) . \displaystyle u(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{f_{x}(-t_{x})}{D(\boldsymbol{x})},\qquad v(\boldsymbol{x})=\frac{f_{y}(-t_{y})}{D(\boldsymbol{x})}.
Rendering the translated pose produces 𝑰 ~ \widetilde{\boldsymbol{I}} , which we warp back via differentiable sampling:
(2)
𝑰 w ​ ( 𝒙 ) = 𝑰 ~ ​ ( 𝒙 + [ u ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) v ⁡ ( 𝒙 ) ] ) . \displaystyle\boldsymbol{I}_{w}(\boldsymbol{x})=\widetilde{\boldsymbol{I}}\!\left(\boldsymbol{x}+\begin{bmatrix}u(\boldsymbol{x})\\
v(\boldsymbol{x})\end{bmatrix}\right).
This optical-flow parameterization explicitly reasons about horizontal and vertical displacements, providing extra robustness along the vertical axis when the training views lie roughly on the same horizontal ring (a common sparse-view setting where vertical supervision is weak). Over the valid pixel set 𝒱 \mathcal{V} (visible and in-bounds), we measure the Charbonnier photometric consistency as
(3)

where ϵ > 0 \epsilon>0 is a small constant.
To regularize depth and the induced flow, we adopt total variation (TV) regularization for both depth and flow fields, along with edge-aware smoothing to preserve depth discontinuities at image edges.

3D hypothesis as the unit of stereo feature aggregation: each hypothesis is projected into both calibrated views, and
the paired features support pose refinement. A second path predicts image-plane corrections, intersects the resulting
viewing rays, and blends this geometric update with a learned update. We first describe this decoder, then the encoder,
temporal context and training objective.

IV-A Task and Data Collection
A bimanual peg-in-hole task - widely regarded as one of the most challenging benchmarks in robotic assembly due to its high precision requirements - is selected for the experimental validation. As shown in Fig. 5 , both the enclosure and the connector are placed at random locations on the table within the robot’s workspace. The objective is for the robot to grasp both objects and precisely insert the connector into the enclosure with a 1 mm tolerance (Fig. 6 ). The task must be completed without dropping either object, demanding highly coordinated and fine-grained bimanual manipulation. To fully evaluate our system and the proposed TEC strategy, three data collection modalities are compared. The first modality follows Conventional Telemanipulation (CT), consistent with prior works [ 9 , 2 , 8 ] , in which the operator fully controls both robot arms and EEs. This mode serves as our experimental baseline. In this setting, the grasping pose varies across episodes, leading to inconsistent initial conditions for the insertion phase. The second modality HT incorporates vision-based path planning for automated grasping, followed by full telemanipulation to complete the insertion. The third modality applies the TEC strategy, in which grasping is performed autonomously and the operator only provides fine adjustments during the insertion phase. For both HT and TEC modalities, only demonstrations after the autonomous grasping stage are collected.

3.1 Problem formulation As illustrated in Figure  2 , V-JEPA Hand reads a raw video clip and
emits, in a single forward pass, the complete bilateral hand trajectory of that
clip together with the camera motion and focal lengths.
The model has two components: a self-supervised video encoder that turns the
clip into a spatio-temporal token grid (Section  3.2 ), and a
query-based trajectory predictor that decodes one output token per frame into
the per-frame state defined below. Section  3.3 describes
our single-stage training with multi-source supervision, combining large-scale
pseudo-labelled video with high-quality data under a shared
training objective.
Figure 2: Overview of V-JEPA Hand. A V-JEPA 2.1 encoder extracts
spatio-temporal features from an egocentric video clip. A ViT predictor jointly
processes these features, one learnable hand-pose query per frame, and an
intrinsics token. The output queries predict bilateral MANO pose, hand
presence probability, and camera motion. The entire clip is
reconstructed in a single forward pass; blue and orange meshes denote the reconstructed
left and right hands, respectively.

D Limitations and Future Work
Benchmark scope.
Comparing ES and SE. In this work, we introduced SENRI, a new perspective on long video understanding that focuses on the nexus between events and their spatial contexts. We instantiated this problem in SENRI-Bench, a benchmark of long, uninterrupted, self-recorded egocentric videos with six task types spanning two complementary directions, Event → \rightarrow Space and Space → \rightarrow Event. Through an extensive evaluation of direct perception, evidence seeking, and persistent memory approaches, we found that existing systems still struggle on this task. Our analysis further revealed two representative limitations of current memory systems.
