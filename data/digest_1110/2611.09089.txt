Hand–Object Interaction (HOI) is an atomic unit of everyday activities.
Logging HOIs provides time-stamped evidence of daily actions, i.e., what
objects the hands interacted with, what actions occurred, and when, enabling
immediate assistance in daily life.
This capability is important because everyday memory slips are both
common and consequential: diary-based studies show that over 60%
of reported memory failures involve forgetting intended
actions  ( Mogle et al., 2023 ) .
As a result, HOI logging can naturally support reminders
(e.g., “Did I lock the door?”), verification (e.g., “Did
I take my medication?”), and rapid retrieval (e.g., “Where
did I leave the charger?”), which correspond to frequent types
of day-to-day lapses.
Beyond memory augmentation, HOI logs can also strengthen health-routine
summarization : the World Health Organization (WHO) reported
that adherence to long-term therapies for chronic diseases averages
only ∼ \sim 50% in developed countries, motivating systems that can
passively verify routine actions without relying on self-report  ( Rassool, 2004 ) .
Recent camera-equipped smartglasses have increased interest in
egocentric HOI logging by providing a wearable, always-available
viewpoint that naturally captures the wearer’s hands and the objects
they manipulate.
This trend is reflected in the rapid growth of large-scale
egocentric HOI datasets and advances in computer vision (CV) algorithms
( Ma and others, 2025 ; Grauman and others, 2022 ; Perrett and others, 2025 ) .

4.3.1. Egomotion Estimation.
Accurate egomotion estimation is challenging on resource-constrained,
battery-powered devices.
A head-mounted IMU available on most smartglasses offers a low-power
option (< 10 10  mW) and has been widely used for human motion estimation  ( Santaera et al., 2015 ; Forster et al., 2017 ) .
In principle, linear displacement and angular rotation can be obtained
by integrating linear acceleration and angular velocity, respectively.
However, integration error is the key challenge: displacement estimation requires double integration of acceleration, which amplifies sensor noise and bias and accumulates into large position drift over time  ( Wang et al., 2015 ; Norrdine et al., 2016 ) .
Existing systems typically address this issue either by fusing camera and IMU
signals, as in VIO and SLAM  ( Forster et al., 2017 ; Campos et al., 2021 ) , or by
using periodic zero-velocity references (ZUPT)  ( Wang et al., 2015 ; Norrdine et al., 2016 ) .
However, camera fusion requires pixel-level matching on high-FPS video (e.g., 20–30 FPS)  ( Campos et al., 2021 ; Project Aria, 2026 ) , adding sensing and compute overhead that conflicts with EventHOI ’s low-power goals.
On the other hand, ZUPT works well for foot-mounted IMUs with stance phases, but is unreliable for head-mounted IMUs that rarely become stationary  ( Wang et al., 2015 ; Norrdine et al., 2016 ) .
Our key idea is to leverage prior knowledge about when informative HOIs
are most likely to occur to guide egomotion estimation.
We observe that meaningful HOIs are rarely associated with large translational
motion of the user, which often reflects whole-body movement rather than
interaction-relevant behavior.
For example, activities such as walking can generate substantial global motion
but seldom correspond to informative HOIs.
By contrast, HOIs typically occur when the body is relatively stable, as
successful object interaction requires coordinated eye, head, and hand
movements  ( Li et al., 2013 ) .
Based on this observation, we decompose egomotion into two categories:
(1) translational body motion , such as walking, which is often accompanied
by few HOIs, and (2) rotational head motion , such as small head turns
while standing or sitting, which commonly co-occurs with HOIs.
Step 1: Translational Body Motion Detector.
We first identify periods of large translational body motion, during which
informative HOIs are unlikely  ( Li et al., 2013 ) .
Based on our empirical study, we define such motion as linear velocity above
0.5 0.5  m/s, since head motion alone rarely exceeds this threshold.
Rather than directly integrating IMU signals, we train a lightweight 2-layer
LSTM binary detector that takes 6-axis IMU data sampled at 50 50  Hz over a
0.5-second window as input.
We train the model using the large-scale egocentric Nymeria dataset, collected
with Meta Aria smartglasses  ( Ma and others, 2025 ; Project Aria, 2026 ) .
Ground-truth labels are derived from SLAM-estimated egomotion velocities, with
windows above 0.5 0.5  m/s labeled positive.
To maintain class balance, we use a 5-hour indoor subset for training.
The detector runs with a default duty cycle of 0.5.
When large translation is detected, EventHOI directly disables RGB recording.
We adopt binary detection rather than precise velocity estimation because exact
translation is unnecessary for HOI triggering and too costly for on-device processing.
Our model contains only 37k parameters (~37 KB of int8 weights) and
requires ~1.8 MFLOPs per 0.5-second window, making it practical for low-power MCU deployment.
Despite this, our model shows a high prediction accuracy of 95.7% and a recall of 0.96 for no-translation-period recognition, which minimizes the likelihood of skipping HOIs.

EventHOI : Event-Driven Resource-Efficient Hand-Object Interaction Logging on Smartglasses
Le Zhang 1 , Kailai Cui 1 , Hao Chen 2 , Vlad Roznyatovskiy 2 , Jianzhong Zhang 2 , Anhong Guo 1 , Kang G. Shin 1 , Ke Sun 1
Affiliation:  1 University of Michigan   2 Samsung Research America
email: zle@umich.edu
© none We presented EventHOI , an event-driven system for resource-efficient egocentric HOI logging on smartglasses.
It uses low-power event sensing and IMU-assisted egomotion cancellation to selectively capture HOI-relevant RGB content without continuous RGB recording or heavyweight on-device computation.
More broadly, EventHOI points to a promising design paradigm for resource-constrained systems: using low-power sensors to guide and sparsify the operation of high-power sensors to enable resource-efficient embedded sensing systems.
