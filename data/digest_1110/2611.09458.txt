【引言】
 A growing number of vision systems need geometrically consistent disparity for both cameras of a stereo rig, not just for a reference view.
Per-eye passthrough rendering on AR/VR headsets reprojects and composites each eye with its own depth  [ 8 ] , so any left-right disagreement introduces noticeable artifacts  [ 12 ] .
Stereoscopic content must also keep disparity within a viewing-comfort zone  [ 23 ] . Novel view synthesis from a stereo pair renders new viewpoints from the two input views  [ 66 ] , so where the two disparity maps disagree the composite tears and ghosts at occlusion boundaries. Two-view depth fusion likewise yields doubled geometry wherever the two estimates disagree. These examples demonstrate the need not just for two accurate depth maps, but also consistent ones.
Figure 1 : Sample predictions with and without our proposed cross-injection (CI) extension. CI can be added to any iterative stereo matcher (here FoundationStereo  [ 56 ] ) and substantially improves left-right consistency between two otherwise independent inference passes (left example); it also reduces per-view failure modes (right example).
Here, we show two examples from the Booster dataset  [ 39 ] where inconsistent predictions arise mainly on reflective and transparent surfaces. Additional visualizations are shown in Figs.  4 , 5 and 6 .
State-of-the-art stereo matchers, however, typically predict only the left (reference) disparity, a convention encouraged by benchmarks that score only the left view  [ 40 , 14 , 35 , 42 ] .
In practice, the standard way to obtain the right disparity from such a network is to horizontally flip and swap the input pair, re-posing the right view as a left-view problem, and run the same weights a second time. The two passes are coupled only implicitly through the image content; nothing enforces that their outputs agree. As a result, they often diverge in textureless regions, at occlusion boundaries, and on transparent or reflective surfaces, where each pass locks onto a different plausible depth (Fig.  1 , left example, middle row).

3.1 Stereo Refinement via Cross-Injection
Our extension applies to any iterative (refinement-based) stereo matcher trained to predict left disparities only  [ 28 , 36 , 56 ] . The right disparity is obtained as in Sec.  1 by flipping and swapping the input pair and flipping the prediction back. Each pass refines its disparity with a RAFT-style  [ 45 , 28 ] convolutional GRU that gathers correlation features at the current estimate and emits an increment; the passes share weights but not state and interact only through the correlation volume, so agreement would be encouraged only indirectly by the disparity loss (Eq.  8 ). Cross-injection instead tells each pass, at every step and pixel, where and by how much it disagrees with the other view, so it can act on the disagreement during refinement.

【结论/局限】
Why does coupling both views improve single-view accuracy? Why does coupling both views improve single-view accuracy?
Beyond test-time agreement, the coupling appears to aid training in three ways:
First, it extends supervision : many stereo datasets label only the left view, so the right pass is otherwise trained only indirectly; coupling the two views turns single-view labels into a learning signal for both.
Second, it acts as a geometric regularizer : requiring the two per-view disparities to agree constrains otherwise under-determined predictions.
Appendix  F shows the constraint acting where it should: the change concentrates where the two views disagree and vanishes where they already agree, offset by a mild regression at occluded pixels, where the constraint does not apply.
Third, the coupling is soft : the signed inconsistency is provided as an input cue rather than a hard constraint, allowing the model to rely on it where it is trustworthy and ignore it where it is not. More broadly, this suggests that supervising only the left view may be the wrong default even when only left-view disparity is sought: future stereo models may benefit from supervising both views in one form or another, whether through coupled per-view passes (such as cross-injection) or a fully symmetric design. .
