【引言】
 LLM-based conversational agents are increasingly capable of changing peoples’ minds: they match human writers on policy arguments  [ 1 , 37 ] , outperform humans in live debates when given basic information about their opponent  [ 42 ] , and can reduce conspiracy beliefs through short dialogues  [ 7 ] .
Personalization may extend beyond personal information, such as demographic characteristics, preferences, to real-time physiological data.
Headsets, watches, and earbuds can stream heart rate, pupil diameter, and related measures while a conversation unfolds.
An LLM could use these signals as additional context when adapting its arguments, without relying on what the user explicitly communicates.
Prior work shows that physiological adaptation can benefit users, for example by enabling coaching systems to respond to stress or fatigue  [ 44 , 21 , 47 , 9 ] . The same capability, however, raises concerns about manipulation when used covertly to increase persuasive influence  [ 8 , 32 , 41 ] . Disclosure offers one safeguard, but it may affect how people evaluate a persuader without changing whether their attitudes shift  [ 29 , 3 ] .
We therefore examine perceived persuasiveness and attitude movement separately, both before and after participants learn that the system can access physiological signals.
Studying these effects requires varying the model’s access to physiological signals without visibly changing the sensing setup. We used sensors integrated into a VR headset that participants wore throughout the study. The physical setup remained unchanged whether or not heart-rate and pupil features were passed to the model.
Twenty-four participants each held four conversations with a GPT-4 Turbo chatbot arguing against a self-selected opinion. The 2 × 2 2\times 2 design crossed sensing (on or off) with awareness (before or after disclosure); in the post-disclosure sensing-off cell, participants believed they were being sensed although no signals were assigned to the model. We ask:
•
RQ1: When physiological sensing is hidden, does access to the signals change perceived persuasiveness and the direction of immediate attitude movement?
•
RQ2: How do these outcomes differ

5.3 Conversational style and structure
We examined whether sensing changed the chatbot’s response style or the structure of the conversation.
Reply length did not differ reliably between conditions (mean sensing-on difference: 9.0 non-space characters, p = .28 p=.28 ).
However, an average of 67.9% of sensing-on replies per conversation contained a question mark, compared with 91.9% of sensing-off replies (difference: 24.0 percentage points, p = .001 p=.001 ). Sensing-on conversations also averaged 0.77 fewer turns ( p = .010 p=.010 ).

Affiliation:  School of Computer Science and Information Technology, Adelaide University, Australia This paper examined whether physiological signals from a VR headset change the persuasiveness of an LLM-based chatbot, both before and after sensing was disclosed.
Before disclosure, participants rated the chatbot as more persuasive when sensing was on. Across the study, sensing was also associated with greater immediate movement toward the chatbot’s position.
Dialogue logs showed a more declarative style when sensing was on, with fewer replies containing question marks and fewer conversational turns.
Persuasiveness ratings were lower after disclosure, although this difference cannot be separated from session order.
These findings motivate making biosignal-driven adaptation inspectable and providing a genuine non-sensed mode.
