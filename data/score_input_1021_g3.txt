3.1. Overview
We propose SynerGest , a high-fidelity co-speech gesture generator built on Contrastive Flow Matching (CFM), designed to model the interaction within a dyadic conversation. The generator synthesizes motion 𝐱 ∈ ℝ T × D \mathbf{x}\in\mathbb{R}^{T\times D} for the target avatar (acting as the listener in our joint-modeling scenario), where T T denotes the sequence length and D D represents the modality-specific spatial dimension. We first extract raw audio waveforms from both the target listener ( 𝐚 l ​ s ​ n \mathbf{a}_{lsn} ) and the conversational partner ( 𝐚 s ​ p ​ k \mathbf{a}_{spk} ). These are denoted as 𝐀 = ( 𝐚 l ​ s ​ n , 𝐚 s ​ p ​ k ) \mathbf{A}=(\mathbf{a}_{lsn},\mathbf{a}_{spk}) and jointly condition generation. A shared, pretrained wav2vec 2.0 backbone combined with mel-spectrogram features processes the two channels independently. To accommodate distinct kinetic properties, the flow estimator separates body and facial-expression pathways (“PoseOnly” and “FaceOnly” in Fig.  2 ). Rather than using a stochastic diffusion chain, we learn a step-size-conditioned velocity field v θ ​ ( 𝐱 t , t , d , 𝐜 ) v_{\theta}(\mathbf{x}_{t},t,d,\mathbf{c}) , where t t is the absolute flow time and d d is the requested integration step size. The field transports samples from a Gaussian prior p 0 ​ ( 𝐱 ) = 𝒩 ​ ( 0 , I ) p_{0}(\mathbf{x})=\mathcal{N}(0,I) to the empirical distribution p 1 ​ ( 𝐱 ) p_{1}(\mathbf{x}) . For fixed initial noise and audio, integrating from t = 0 t=0 to 1 1 gives a deterministic ODE trajectory; drawing a fresh initial sample yields a different plausible gesture for the same audio.
3.2. Conditional Feature Generation and Fusion
As illustrated in our overall architecture, the conditional pipeline follows a structured progression from raw unimodal extraction to multimodal interactive alignment. These embeddings are concatenated to jointly encode the dyadic conversational context, forming the base acoustic feature 𝐅 a \mathbf{F}_{a} . Crucially, to accommodate their distinct kinetic properties, our framework adopts a decoupled extraction strategy for body pose and facial expressions. Datasets. We evaluate the proposed SynerGest framework on two extensive dyadic interaction datasets: the Audio2Photoreal dataset  ( Ng and others, 2024 ) and Seamless Interaction  ( Agrawal and others, 2025 ) . Audio2Photoreal offers high-fidelity, studio-captured dyadic conversations paired with photorealistic Codec Avatar assets. The acoustic input comprises 48 kHz dual-channel audio, explicitly capturing the independent waveforms of both the speaker and the listener. Additionally, we utilize the Naturalistic subset of the Seamless Interaction dataset, which encompasses unscripted, spontaneous recordings. For Seamless, the release provides a single-channel track for each participant. Using the interaction-partner mapping, we pair the target and partner tracks, resample them to 48 kHz, align them to their common duration, and stack them as a dual-channel input. Metrics. We adopt four metrics to comprehensively evaluate our framework: Fréchet Gesture Distance (FGD ↓ \downarrow ) for kinematic realism, Beat Consistency (BC ↑ \uparrow ) for audio-motion synchronization, Diversity (Div ↑ \uparrow ) for motion variance, and Inference Efficiency ( 𝒯 inf \mathcal{T}_{\text{inf}} ↓ \downarrow ), defined as the average wall-clock inference time per sample.
Baselines. To rigorously demonstrate the superiority of our framework, we compare SynerGest against four recent state-of-the-art co-speech gesture generation methods. Specifically, we evaluate against three advanced monadic frameworks—EMAGE  ( Liu and others, 2024 ) , GestureLSM  ( Liu et al., 2025b ) , and TalkSHOW  ( Yi and others, 2023 ) —alongside one dedicated dyadic baseline, the Audio2Photoreal (A2P) model  ( Ng and others, 2024 ) . To ensure a fair comparison, all monadic baselines are adapted to our dyadic evaluation protocol under identical data processing conditions.

3.3 Patch-aligned Sub-pixel Gaussian Reconstruction Overview. FineSplat first performs view-wise grouping and applies self-attention within each group, enabling efficient processing of fine-grained tokens. 3D spatial grouping lifts tokens into 3D space and enables interactions among spatially neighboring tokens, including those from different view groups, to enhance cross-view information exchange. Compact Gaussian generation predicts fewer Gaussians per token than existing pixel-aligned methods, maintaining a compact 3DGS representation. Finally, direct error feedback incorporates reconstruction errors obtained by rendering the input views to further refine token representations. Given N N context views with or without known camera poses, FineSplat predicts a set of 3D Gaussians in a feed-forward manner.
Stage 1 divides each context view into fine-grained 4 × 4 4\times 4 patches and encodes them with attention of varying scope, where group-wise attention over a few views keeps fine-grained multi-view reasoning affordable.
Stage 2 decodes these tokens into an initial set of Gaussians, renders them
back into the context views, and uses the resulting error maps to update the tokens through 3D spatial grouping, which lets tokens that fall in different view groups interact once they are lifted to 3D.
This dual-grouping strategy makes fine-grained encoding tractable, with 3D spatial grouping complementing view-wise grouping by connecting tokens across view groups. Figure  2 gives an overview of the two stages.
3.1 Stage 1: View-wise token grouping

3.1 Gaussian Representation
3.1.1 3D Gaussian Splatting
3D Gaussian Splatting (3DGS) Kerbl et al. (2023) represents a scene with a set of anisotropic 3D Gaussian primitives placed in 3D space. Each Gaussian is parameterized by a centre 𝐱 i ∈ ℝ 3 \mathbf{x}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} , a scaling vector 𝐬 i ∈ ℝ 3 \mathbf{s}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} , a rotation quaternion 𝐪 i ∈ ℝ 4 \mathbf{q}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{4} , an opacity α i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] \alpha_{i}\in[0,1] , and appearance-related attributes. The scaling vector and rotation quaternion together define the Gaussian covariance. The trainable parameters of the i i -th Gaussian are
G i = { 𝐱 i , 𝐬 i , 𝐪 i , α i , ϕ i } , \displaystyle G_{i}=\{\mathbf{x}_{i},\mathbf{s}_{i},\mathbf{q}_{i},\alpha_{i},\phi_{i}\},
(1)
where ϕ i \phi_{i} denotes the appearance representation of the Gaussian.
Because the Gaussian primitives are defined in 3D space, the appearance attributes of a Gaussian are shared by every viewpoint that observes the same spatial region. This gives a fixed correspondence between scene geometry and appearance during rendering, so a change to a Gaussian’s appearance attributes affects all views of that region in the same way, which is useful for keeping a stylization consistent across viewpoints.

Specifically,
ϕ i = { 𝐚 i , r i , m i , 𝐧 i } , \displaystyle\phi_{i}=\{\mathbf{a}_{i},r_{i},m_{i},\mathbf{n}_{i}\},
(2)
where 𝐚 i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] 3 \mathbf{a}_{i}\in[0,1]^{3} is the diffuse albedo of the i i -th Gaussian, r i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] r_{i}\in[0,1] is the roughness, m i ∈ [ 0 , 1 ] m_{i}\in[0,1] is the metallicity, and 𝐧 i ∈ ℝ 3 \mathbf{n}_{i}\in\mathbb{R}^{3} is the surface normal.
In an RGB appearance representation a single per-point colour already combines material reflectance, the illumination together with the shading it produces, and view-dependent effects. The decomposition above instead stores the albedo 𝐚 i \mathbf{a}_{i} as a separate per-Gaussian coefficient, alongside r i r_{i} , m i m_{i} , 𝐧 i \mathbf{n}_{i} , and the global lighting L L .

4.1 Experimental Setup
Datasets. We evaluate RxGS from two angles: cross-view geometric consistency and relightable stylization. We first compare RxGS with existing reference-based 3D scene stylization methods using a geometric consistency metric, which measures how well scene structure is preserved across viewpoints after stylization. Because the existing methods do not support relighting, we then evaluate the relightable stylization of RxGS by rendering stylized scenes under different illumination conditions and examining how the appearance responds.
4.1 Experimental Setup
Datasets.
We use NeRF-Synthetic Mildenhall et al. (2021) and NeRF-LLFF Mildenhall et al. (2019) for the geometric consistency comparison, and add TensorIR Jin et al. (2023) and Mip-NeRF 360 Barron et al. (2022) for the relightable stylization experiments. NeRF-Synthetic Mildenhall et al. (2021) is a synthetic multi-view dataset of eight object-centric scenes, each with 100 training images and 200 test images rendered at 800 × 800 800\times 800 , with camera poses and foreground masks. NeRF-LLFF Mildenhall et al. (2019) is a real-world forward-facing multi-view dataset captured by handheld cameras with calibrated poses; it contains more complex geometry, occlusions, and appearance variation than NeRF-Synthetic, and we downsample the input images by a factor of four, following prior work Zhang et al. (2023) ; Fridovich-Keil et al. (2022) ; Tang et al. (2023) . TensorIR Jin et al. (2023) is a multi-view dataset for inverse rendering and relighting, with diverse materials and illumination and with ground-truth geometry, material, and lighting; we use its Lego and Hotdog scenes for relightable stylization. Mip-NeRF 360 Barron et al. (2022) is a real-world multi-view dataset of unbounded indoor and outdoor scenes captured over 360-degree viewpoints, with larger extents and more complex structure than NeRF-Synthetic and LLFF; we use its Garden , Kitchen , and Bicycle scenes. 4.4.1 Effect of the Style Supervision Strategy Protocol.
To reduce bias toward a particular artistic appearance, we evaluate each scene with three different reference styles, and for the geometric consistency comparison we average the reported results over the three styles. For relighting, NeRF-Synthetic and LLFF scenes are rendered under several illumination conditions by rotating the estimated environment map while keeping all stylization parameters fixed. III-E Reconstruction
Our reconstruction consists of COLMAP and 3DGS processes, both adjusted for better accuracy.
III-E 1 Bundle Adjustment
We use COLMAP  [ 26 , 27 ] to refine the camera intrinsics and extrinsics, and to generate a sparse point cloud for initializing the 3DGS optimization.
First, SIFT  [ 19 ] features are extracted and exhaustively matched across all views.
COLMAP’s incremental mapper is then seeded with our initial camera intrinsics and the robot’s reported end-effector poses.
We let COLMAP refine these further, accounting for errors in intrinsics, as well as small, unmodeled pose offsets from the custom microscope mount.

4.4 Augmented Reality 4.1 Image Generation
Image generation was performed based on DreamBooth [ 7 ] , a method to finetune a latent diffusion model to a particular object or identity. We based our implementation on the DreamBooth implementation by Shivam Shrirao 3 3
3
https://github.com/ShivamShrirao/diffusers which is based on [ 9 ] but in addition provides a straightforward Jupyter notebook that was deployed on Google Colab. As pre-trained models we choose Stable Diffusion 1.5 as well as Stable Diffusion 2.1 as both of them are favorable depending on the decade we are training on. This also matches the experience many artists and researchers had, that depending on the specific goal the specific choices of the two models (e.g. the encoder and training diet) lead to different behavior.
In our case we did not aim to reproduce a specific identity or object but rather a specific style of an artist but the DreamBooth fine-tuning strategy still performed very well. We changed two parameters of the implementation. First we removed the prior preservation. This strategy usually ensures that the model is still able to generalize after finetuning; however, in our case, it leads to the undesired effect that the generated images are not adopting the style of the artist (as the loss forces it to generate images in the original style). Due to this change our model however also misses generalization capabilities, which means that since we are only training a comparable small set of images without language annotation, the model also looses its capability to generate images that fit a specific text prompt. We view this as feature, as the image generation for our model was not biased by a human induced text prompt. The second small change we made to better adopt the style was to increase the maximal number of training steps from 800 to 10’000 which leads to better style adoption.
For both those changes we did not perform extensive qualitative or quantitative evaluation but rather tuned them based on our own qualitative observation on a small subset of generated images. The release of part of our training data enables a more structured comparison of different and newer models as well as more modern fine-tuning techniques. We did not perform such an evaluation as it was not the focus of this project, we do however see already challenges in the choice or design of metrics to evaluate generated artwork in terms of style and content.
Whilst for our photographs proper color calibration is available, Stable Diffusion does not distinguish color spaces and assumes sRGB. For our specific project this is not a limitation, as the generation through the artists anyway adds a creative component with some artistic freedom.

5.1 Image Generation
We finetuned model variants on 5 different training sets, always on one decade of artwork of Hans Furer. We experimented with both Stable Diffusion 1.5 as well as Stable Diffusion 2.1. resulting in a total of 10 model variants. The resulting pieces for the decade from 1981-1990 and 1991-2000 can be seen in Figure  5 and Figure  6 respectively. The style of Hans Furer changes quite a bit over time and we observed that the two different models behave differently and depending on the decade generated artwork of one of the models was favored. Since both models share the same basis images generated with the same seed often share quite some similarity, which enables a direct comparison between the models. For the decade from 1981-1990, where Hans Furer drew many faces and bodies the newer model leads to much more detail and makes the images less blurry. For the decade from 1991-2000, which contains much more abstract images in the training data, the newer model seems to struggle more. The key observable difference from our perspective is that the newer model tends to have more texture-like patterns, which could either be a bias inherited by the difference in training data, or a case of hallucination by the model. In contrast, the artwork of Hans Furer is often more calm in the background. For each decade, one images was manually chosen by Bernhard Egger and Hans Furer based on personal preferences. This piece was then transferred to canvas and paired with a piece from the training data that was selected by the artist. The final selection of generated artworks is shown in Figure  7 .
