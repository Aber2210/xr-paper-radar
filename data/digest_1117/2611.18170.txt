【引言】
 Geometric constraints are fundamental to multiple-view geometry  [ 48 ] , with image correspondences providing the basis for many of its core estimation problems. Affine correspondences (ACs) enrich point correspondences (PCs) with the local 2 × 2 2\times 2 affine transformation between the corresponding image regions, providing four additional degrees of freedom (DoF) per match. This additional information enables minimal solvers with far fewer correspondences: for example, relative pose estimation requires 5 PCs  [ 67 ] , but only 2 ACs  [ 13 ] . ACs have similarly been exploited for surface-normal estimation  [ 47 ] , homography estimation  [ 12 ] , epipolar geometry  [ 49 , 71 , 20 , 70 , 13 ] , absolute pose  [ 54 , 89 ] , and focal length estimation  [ 18 , 87 ] .
Despite these advances in using ACs, estimating ACs themselves has received little attention. Traditional approaches rely on hand-crafted affine region detectors, such as Harris-Affine  [ 62 ] and Hessian-Affine  [ 63 ] , followed by iterative refinement such as Baumberg iteration  [ 23 ] . Learned approaches later improved this pipeline, most notably AffNet  [ 66 ] . However, since its publication in 2018, progress in AC estimation has been limited, while PC estimation has seen many innovations.
Modern PC estimators reason over global context within and across views, rather than treating correspondences as independent local patches. Detector-free methods such as DKM  [ 33 ] and RoMa  [ 38 , 37 ] , as well as learned sparse matchers such as SuperGlue  [ 75 ] and LoMa  [ 68 ] , have substantially improved the quality and robustness of PCs. Yet AC estimation has largely remained disconnected from this progress. To our knowledge, Sun et al. [81]  is the only approach that combines a modern matcher with learned affine estimation. However, the affine transformation is still predicted from a small local patch around each match, and its four DoF are not predicted. Instead, one network estimates scale and orientation, and a separate one the affine shape.
This raises a natural question: can AC estimation benefit from the same contextual information that has improved PC estimation? Existing approaches reduce each correspondence to a local patch, discarding the image-wide and cross-view information already available in modern matchers. Moreover, the four components of an AC are coupled, making their joint prediction challenging from local appearance alone. 3.3 Affine Head Architecture
For every detected keypoint, the affine head takes the output descriptors of LoMa, and passes them through K = 3 K=3 transformer layers of alternating self- and cross-attention. A per-keypoint MLP then regresses five numbers for the four DoF of Eq.   3 : scale (1), orientation (2, a direction vector normalized to unit length, which avoids the wraparound of regressing an angle directly), and shape (2, the stretch a a and the shear b b ), to compose 𝖠 ^ \hat{\mathsf{A}} . To keep 𝖠 ^ \hat{\mathsf{A}} , and hence its inverse in Eq.   2 , well conditioned, the scale λ = e Δ tanh ( ⋅ ) \lambda=e^{\Delta\tanh(\cdot)} and the stretch a = Δ tanh ( ⋅ ) a=\Delta\tanh(\cdot) are bounded with fixed Δ = 2 \Delta=2 , while the shear b b is regressed directly. The final layer of the prediction head is zero-initialized, so every keypoint starts with an identity affine frame. Because the head attends across views, the per-keypoint affine frames are conditioned on the partner image. We analyze this in Sec.   5.2 and propose in the Suppl. ( Appendix   F ) a method to obtain stable per-view affine frames, useful for e.g .  Structure-from-Motion (SfM).

A Implementation Details We revisit AC estimation and go beyond local patches. Our formulation allows a simpler pipeline that directly regresses the full 4-DoF affine matrix. Systematic ablations show that global context is crucial for affine estimation. Our method, 𝖠 ⋅ \mathsf{A}\cdot LoMa, adds a lightweight affine head to LoMa’s matcher, achieving an order of magnitude lower affine error than AffNet and Sun et al. , with consistent downstream gains.
