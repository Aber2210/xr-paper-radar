Arbitrary-scale light field (LF) spatial super-resolution (LFSSR) reconstructs high-resolution sub-aperture images (SAIs) at a requested spatial scale from low-resolution multi-view observations, while retaining the input viewpoint grid.
The views observe common surfaces at displaced image locations  [ 33 ] , providing complementary samples for recovering spatial detail  [ 1 ] .
Recent encoders aggregate spatial and angular information into low-resolution features  [ 50 , 34 , 35 , 54 ] , which a decoder maps to high-resolution views.
Fixed-factor upsamplers support only predefined upsampling factors.
Coordinate-based implicit decoders allow arbitrary scales by predicting image values from queried coordinates and encoded features  [ 6 , 9 , 11 ] .
However, reconstruction requires neural queries over the high-resolution grid of every view.
Explicit 2D Gaussian decoders instead predict primitives from image features and splat them at the requested resolution  [ 5 , 42 , 24 ] .
Each primitive contributes to multiple output pixels, allowing its parameters to be reused across spatial samples.
For light fields, common scene structure also motivates sharing primitives across views.
A direct extension of a single-image Gaussian decoder applies it separately to each SAI, producing view-specific primitive sets ( Fig.   1 a).
Even when the input features contain multi-view information, these sets do not share primitive identities or geometric parameters.
Common scene structure is therefore parameterized separately, and the primitive count grows with the number of views.
We introduce MacroGS, a feed-forward decoder that jointly predicts Gaussian primitive sets and shares them across output views.
It changes the generation unit from an individual SAI pixel to a macro pixel : the samples at one low-resolution spatial coordinate across the U × V U\times V views ( Fig.   1 b).

Table   1 compares eight encoders with their original upsamplers and with MacroGS. Baseline results are from BasicLFSR  [ 49 ] and, for RASLF, from  [ 54 ] .
For Tab.   2 , we follow the SEIIF  [ 9 ] protocol, combining DistgSSR and EPIT with SEIIF and with view-wise LIIF  [ 6 ] , LTE  [ 32 ] , DIIF  [ 19 ] and CiaoSR  [ 2 ] . PSNR, FLOPs and parameter counts of these combinations and the baselines are from SEIIF, and those of SAEIIF from  [ 11 ] . We also apply GSASR  [ 5 ] view by view, instantiating the per-view Gaussian sets of Fig.   1 a, and train it with the same data and training strategy as MacroGS. All these methods are timed on a single Tesla V100 with a 5 × 5 × 32 × 32 5\times 5\times 32\times 32 input.
4.2 Comparison with existing upsamplers
Generality across encoders.
MacroGS improves the average PSNR of all eight encoders at both factors, with × 4 \times 4 gains of 0.34–0.70 dB and improvements on every dataset for every encoder ( Tab.   1 ).
These results show that MacroGS can improve reconstruction even when the encoder already aggregates spatial and angular information.
For every encoder, the gain is larger at × 4 \times 4 than at × 2 \times 2 .
STFgantry, which has the widest disparity range, receives the largest gain for most encoders, while gains on the narrow-baseline Lytro datasets are generally smaller at × 2 \times 2 .
Arbitrary-scale comparison.
Against GSASR, which predicts a separate primitive set for each view, MacroGS is more accurate at every factor with both encoders while using far fewer parameters and decoding an order of magnitude faster ( Tab.   2 ); sharing one set across views thus comes with no loss in accuracy.
Among all compared decoders, MacroGS is the most accurate in eight of the ten encoder–scale settings and close to the best in the other two, and at in-scale factors its advantage over SEIIF grows with the factor.
MacroGS also decodes fastest at every factor, and its latency grows only slowly with the factor, whereas implicit decoders slow down as the number of queried output coordinates increases. At × 8 \times 8 it is about three times as fast as SEIIF, although its FLOPs are higher, mainly because of the HR residual branch ( Sec.   4.5 ).
In Fig.   3 , errors concentrate around object boundaries and grow with the factor. GSASR leaves the largest boundary errors and more scattered errors in flat regions, and MacroGS reduces both, most visibly at the lower factors.
In Fig.   4 , LFs first downsampled spatially by a factor of 4 are super-resolved at progressively larger factors that are not used in training. At × 16 \times 16 , MacroGS keeps the converging lines of the resolution chart apart over a longer span than GSASR and SEIIF ( Fig.   4(b) ).

4.3 Learned disparity and scene geometry
MacroGS learns disparity only through reconstruction, so we examine its correlation with scene geometry.
At × 4 \times 4 , we average the two components of each primitive disparity and take the mean over the K K primitives of a macro pixel, weighted by central-view opacity.
The reference disparity is the ground truth on HCIold and an SPO estimate  [ 58 ] on HCInew.
Each macro pixel covers 4 × 4 4\times 4 HR pixels at this scale, so we average the reference over them.
We compare the two at textured locations, where the central-view gradient magnitude exceeds the scene median.
Pearson’s r r measures linear correlation, invariant to positive rescaling and additive offsets; Spearman’s ρ \rho measures rank correlation, indicating how well relative depth ordering is preserved.
The mean disparity maps have r r and ρ \rho of at least 0.85 on both datasets, including HCIold with ground-truth disparity ( Tab.   3 ).
This correlation holds across convolutional (DistgSSR), Transformer-based (EPIT) and state-space (RASLF) encoders, with RASLF attaining the highest values on both datasets.
These strong correlations indicate that the learned disparities capture meaningful geometric structure, although they do not imply metrically accurate disparity, consistent with prior observations that geometry learned through reconstruction is optimized for the reconstruction objective  [ 27 , 3 ] .

Affiliation:  Capital Normal University We presented MacroGS, an arbitrary-scale LFSSR decoder that represents each macro pixel with a Gaussian set shared across views.
This design makes the primitive count independent of the number of views, while per-primitive disparities guide both iterative cross-view feature reading and view-conditioned rendering.
Learned solely through reconstruction, these disparities exhibit strong correlations with scene geometry.
MacroGS improves all eight evaluated encoders over their original upsamplers, achieves the highest PSNR in eight of the ten arbitrary-scale settings, and provides a 10 10 – 18 × 18\times decoding speedup over our view-wise GSASR adaptation.
These results support shared, disparity-guided Gaussian primitives as an effective representation for accurate and efficient light field spatial super-resolution.
Future work will explore content-adaptive allocation of shared Gaussian primitives to better represent geometric boundaries and fine structures within a fixed primitive budget.
