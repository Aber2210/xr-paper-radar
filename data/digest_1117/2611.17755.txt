【引言】
 Dense hand contact maps serve as a foundational visual primitive for physical interaction systems. Dexterous robotic manipulation policies require precise knowledge of which finger surfaces will touch an object  Grady et al. (2021) , while immersive AR/VR and digital-human pipelines rely on dense contact to re-target plausible grasps. Although specialized hardware—such as instrumented gloves  Brahmbhatt et al. (2019) —can collect accurate contact labels in controlled laboratory settings, they cannot scale to unconstrained, in-the-wild images. Fortunately, dense hand contact is a property human observers can confidently infer from sight alone: given a still image of a hand interacting with a tool, one can easily distinguish which knuckles press the surface and which finger pads are lifted. This cognitive capability makes per-vertex contact prediction directly from a single RGB image —estimating a binary label for every vertex of a parametric MANO  Romero et al. (2017) hand—a desirable yet challenging visual task.
Figure 1: Dense hand contact paradigms. (a) Geometric proximity after reconstruction. (b) Shared-query direct prediction. (c) VACO samples image evidence at projected vertices.
To date, two primary paradigms have tackled this image-to-contact problem, yet both leave critical cues unexploited. Reconstruction-based approaches  Grady et al. (2021) , also called reconstruct-then-query , first recover both the hand mesh and the interacting object, deriving contact from geometric proximity. While semantically explicit, this paradigm inherits the brittleness of full-object reconstruction, whose errors compound in cluttered scenes. Conversely, direct prediction methods regress per-vertex contact straight from pixels  Tse et al. (2022) ; Hu et al. (2024) . The strongest representative, HACO  Jung and Lee (2025) , achieves state-of-the-art performance by addressing data imbalance through a vertex-level class-balanced loss. However, these fast and scalable predictors treat the entire vertex set as a flat, 1D topological target. By reading every per-vertex output through a single shared contact query token—cross-attending to a spatial image feature grid and decoded by one shared linear head—the network has no per-vertex handle on the image evidence. The model never knows where on the image plane each vertex actually projects. 3.3 Vertex-Aligned Decoding and Multi-Granularity Readout
The sampled feature for each of the V V vertices is projected into a D D -dimensional token by a shallow MLP, and a learned per-vertex embedding 𝐞 v \mathbf{e}_{v} , indexed by the fixed MANO vertex order, is added: 𝐭 v ( 0 ) = MLP ⁡ ( 𝐟 v vit ) + 𝐞 v \mathbf{t}^{(0)}_{v}=\mathrm{MLP}(\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{vit}}_{v})+\mathbf{e}_{v} . Tokens are kept in MANO vertex order, so each carries both its local appearance and the identity of the vertex it describes. These tokens are then fed into an N N -layer Transformer decoder with H H attention heads and hidden dimension D D . Hand-object interactions are structured; for instance, a power grasp involves strong correlations among all fingers, whereas a pinch relies on isolated fingertips. Each layer therefore performs two complementary reads. Self-attention runs globally over the set of V V vertex tokens, letting the decoder capture and propagate these structural contact patterns without hand-crafted heuristic rules. Cross-attention then queries the encoder’s native-stride feature map 𝐅 vit \mathbf{F}_{\mathrm{vit}} , flattened into key/value tokens, so that a vertex can also condition on scene context beyond its own projected footprint—the interacting object, the opposing hand, or an occluder in front of it—which the point-wise sample at its projection cannot carry.

We introduced VACO, grounding dense contact predictions in image features sampled at projected MANO vertices. Joint reconstruction and contact training lets the backbone predict these locations at inference. One checkpoint sets a new state of the art across hand–object, hand–human, hand–scene, and bimanual benchmarks and improves downstream HOI reconstruction with AGILE.
Two limits are worth naming. Accuracy remains bounded by the predicted scaffold: under heavy occlusion or truncation, pose and contact errors are coupled, and the perturbation sweep of Table  4 covers only the measured regime. Grounding is also confined to the image plane: vertices whose projections coincide—most notably palmar and dorsal surfaces—sample identical image features. The learned per-vertex embedding lets such vertices produce different outputs, but the decoder receives no explicit depth, normal or visibility cue, so strongly overlapping surfaces remain ambiguous.
