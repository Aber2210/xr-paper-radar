I-B Design Goal and Contributions I-A Application and Motivation
Manipulating a physical model gives students a way to connect spatial transformations with their mathematical representations. Earlier desktop versions of AR-Classroom used a webcam to display an augmented view of the model on a computer screen. A student could rotate the model about a specified axis and observe the corresponding changes in its coordinate frame, rotation angle, and transformation matrix  [ 1 ] .
The project has since extended this activity to head-mounted mixed reality  [ 2 ] . In a Meta Quest 3 prototype, students can view a physical airplane LEGO model and its virtual representation together as the model moves. In this setting, the overlay must stay aligned as both the model and the student’s viewpoint change. Once the action is complete, the system needs a pose estimate accurate enough to compare the model’s position and orientation with a target pose.
On a standalone headset, camera processing, object tracking, and scene rendering share the same computing resources. This constraint motivates our preliminary investigation of low-compute, markerless tracking of the six-degree-of-freedom (6DoF) pose of a known physical model using the Meta Quest 3’s rendered passthrough view for one eye and onboard processing.
I-B Design Goal and Contributions
Our design goal is to estimate the pose of a known physical model from the passthrough view for one eye while leaving sufficient computing resources for mixed reality rendering on a standalone headset.
1.
Synthetic training data. We developed a Unity  [ 3 ] pipeline that renders the teaching model under varied viewpoints, lighting, part configurations, occlusion, and motion blur. It automatically annotates 20 object keypoints and their visibility in each image.
2.
On-device tracking. We implemented a tracking pipeline on the Meta Quest 3 that updates a crop around the object and resizes it to a 128 × 128 128\times 128 RGB input. A compact YOLO model trained on the synthetic dataset predicts the 2D keypoint locations. A perspective- n n -point (PnP) solver estimates the object’s pose for mixed reality visualization using these predictions and the corresponding known 3D points.
3.
Preliminary evaluation. We report 2D detection and
keypoint localization results on synthetic validation images and
runtime measurements on the Meta Quest 3. A qualitative headset
demonstration shows how the estimated 6DoF pose drives a mixed
reality overlay as a user rotates the physical airplane.

III-A Task Definition and Overview
We estimate the 6DoF pose of a known LEGO airplane model from the Meta Quest 3’s rendered passthrough view for one eye. The pose consists of a rotation 𝐑 \mathbf{R} and a translation 𝐭 \mathbf{t} that map points from the airplane’s reference frame to the camera frame. The system uses the camera pose associated with each image to express the estimated object pose in the headset’s tracking space. AR-Classroom can then use this pose to align virtual overlays with the airplane and compare its final pose with a target.
Figure  1 shows the connection between offline training and on-device tracking. We render the airplane in Unity and generate keypoint annotations to train the YOLO model offline. On the headset, the tracker crops the current camera image and resizes the crop to a 128 × 128 128\times 128 RGB input. The trained model predicts the image locations of the airplane’s landmarks. The system maps these predictions back to the camera image coordinates and estimates the pose with PnP using the corresponding 3D landmarks and camera intrinsics. The detected bounding box and the validity of the pose estimate guide the crop for the next frame.

III-B Synthetic Data Generation and Training
We generated 50,000 source images at 640 × 640 640\times 640 pixels in Unity using a digital model of the LEGO airplane. To reflect the indoor settings in which learners handle the model, we drew approximately 90% of the backgrounds from the classroom, living room, and library categories of the indoorCVPR_09 dataset  [ 9 ] . The remaining backgrounds were photographs taken in the project’s intended use settings.
Following the principle of domain randomization  [ 10 ] ,
we varied the airplane’s position and orientation, lighting, part
removal and rotation, and motion blur. We placed capsule-shaped objects between the camera and the airplane to approximate the partial occlusion caused by fingers during handling. We set a 10% probability of rendering a scene without the airplane. When the airplane was included, we set a 30% probability of sampling a view near one of its six principal directions. These probabilities governed scene generation, so the realized proportions could vary across batches.
For scenes containing the airplane, we projected 20 predefined 3D landmarks into the image and checked whether each point fell within the frame and whether it was occluded. The standard YOLO Pose annotations were stored in separate text files containing the class ID, normalized bounding box, and landmark coordinates and visibility flags. Images without the airplane and images with fewer than two landmarks within the frame were retained as negative samples with empty annotation files.
We split the source renders into training and validation sets at an 80:20 ratio before preparing the cropped views. We retained full-frame examples and used a preset sampling rate of 80% to generate additional crops around the annotated bounding boxes. All versions of a source render remained in the same split, and bounding boxes and landmark coordinates were remapped to the crop. The two types of views expose the model to the range of object scales encountered during wide-view search and close-up tracking on the headset. We resized the resulting images to 128 × 128 128\times 128 pixels for RGB training of the YOLO26n-pose model  [ 11 ] .
