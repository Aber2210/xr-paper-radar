【引言】
 Human demonstrations are the easiest dexterous manipulation data to collect at scale, and real-to-sim-to-real pipelines rebuild them in simulation, where one recording seeds many ( Wang et al., 2026 ; Mao et al., 2026 ; Choi et al., 2026 ; Dan et al., 2025 ; Allshire et al., 2025 ) . However, these pipelines rebuild the interaction from vision, which recovers only how the hand moves, how the object moves, or both. Contact force never enters the simulation, since video cannot observe it ( Zhou et al., 2026b ) . A hand pose shows where each finger stops, not how far it closes, which sets grip force, and contacts are often hidden. Video thus misses which fingers touch, when, and how hard, and its two standard uses fail in opposite directions. In our experiments, pose imitation grips with 0.31 × \times the recorded force, and replays built from vision with 1.55 × \times . Physics alone does not recover the human grip either. In our touch-grounded replays of the mug pick-up task, the recorded grip is 1.6–3.7 × \times the smallest force required to hold the mug. Touch measures this quantity ( Sundaram et al., 2019 ; Yang et al., 2022 ; Song et al., 2025 ) .
Gloves and other wearables have been used to model hand-object dynamics ( Zhang et al., 2021 ) , map touch to and from video ( Zeng et al., 2026a ; Zhang et al., 2026b ; Zeng et al., 2026b ) , supervise robot policies ( Luo et al., 2026 ; Ni et al., 2026 ; Yu et al., 2024 ; Xu et al., 2025 ) and identify object dynamics ( Engelbracht et al., 2026 ) , but none rebuilds the hand-object interaction as a physics simulation driven by measured touch, which lets one recording be replayed under new conditions. Touch predicted from video ( Zhou et al., 2026b ; Zhang et al., 2026a ; Zhou et al., 2026a ) is trained to reproduce glove signals from the same pixels, and it cannot recover a force the pixels do not show or change the conditions of a recording. In contrast, TouchTwin rebuilds each selected TouchScale ( Li et al., 2026 ) recording in MuJoCo ( Todorov et al., 2012 ) , as shown in Figure 1 , enabling scalable human-touch data generation. Coding agents write objects as physically tested programs (Fig. 3 ), a hand model per subject is tracked from both cameras ( Potamias et al., 2024 ) (Fig. 2 ), and the recorded touch closes each finger past the visible pose to set where, when, and how hard it presses (Fig. 4 ). 4 Downstream Applications
Each replay also seeds perturbed demonstrations with simulated contact and force labels, rendered as realistic video in new views and scenes.
Figure 6 : Perturbed replays of one recording. (a) Other hand postures with the same grip effort; under each, the simulated touch of that frame on the glove’s pad layout. The recorded grasp, thumb-index pinch, top claw and side wrap carry the mug (drift 3.8, 2.1, 1.9 and 5.1 mm in the hand); with the rim-wall pinch the mug swings 73° in the hand and lands 36 mm off target. (b) Perturbations of motion, grip and object. A 12 × \times faster carry slips the mug 19 mm in the hand (the green outline is the mug in the recorded grip); the grip fades until the mug slips at 22% of the recorded grip and drops; with 300 g of water the mug needs 27.4 N to hold against 20.8 N empty (+31%); at 0.12 × \times friction it slips and drops; a 4 Hz shake makes the hand let go at ± \pm 60 mm (4.7 g).
Physics turns one recording into labeled successes and failures. A perturbation changes one thing in a replay and re-runs the physics: the grasp (another hand posture at the same grip effort), the motion (a faster carry or an added shake), the grip (each finger’s force target scaled down) or the object (heavier, or more slippery). With nothing changed, it reproduces the replay exactly. Because physics resolves the outcome, the mug can slip, swing or drop, and every perturbed replay carries simulated per-taxel touch, contact and force in the glove’s format (Fig. 6 ). Sweeping one factor until the object drops measures how much margin the recorded grasp had (Fig. 7 ).

Figure 7 : Perturbed until the mug drops. During the carry, (a) every finger’s force target is turned down from 1 × \times to 0, (b) the hand-mug friction is turned down, (c) mass is added up to 7.5 kg (far more than water could add), and (d) a 4 Hz side-to-side shake grows in amplitude. Each sweep runs until the mug leaves the hand.
One replay becomes realistic video in new views and scenes. Wan2.2 ( Team Wan et al., 2025 ) renders a replay or perturbed replay as realistic video, conditioned on the simulation along the recorded head-camera trajectory. Changing the camera, the hand posture or the scene gives the same simulated interaction from other viewpoints, with other grasps and in other places, and each generated video inherits the contact, touch and force labels of its replay (Fig. 8 ).
Figure 8 : Generated videos decoded from touch-grounded replays (one frame each). From left: a generated video in the recording’s own view and scene; a wrist camera in the same scene; new viewpoints, hand postures and scenes.
Simulation measures touch where the glove has no sensors. The glove does not cover the whole hand: the centre of the palm and parts of the finger bases have no sensors. The simulated hand has skin everywhere, so a replay records contact that the glove cannot. Figure 9 perturbs the mug replay so that the palm presses onto the mug: the glove-layout palm taxels stay at zero, while the simulator reports the normal force on palm skin without taxels.
