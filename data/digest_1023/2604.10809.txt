3.6 Policy Training WARPED transforms
RGB egocentric human demonstrations into wrist-camera robotic observation-action datasets to train visuomotor policies for tabletop manipulation tasks. An overview of our pipeline is shown in Fig.  2 . The pipeline consists of five stages: data collection, interactive scene initialization, hand-object optimization, wrist-view retargeting and rendering, and policy training and deployment. The system is designed to be portable and easy to use, enabling demonstration collection using only a single monocular RGB camera
without specialized hardware or sensing.
3.1 Assumptions
We primarily consider rigid-object tabletop manipulation tasks with a gripper end-effector and without significant scene changes beyond the hand-object interaction. The manipulated object is assumed to remain partially visible, avoiding full occlusions that would prevent tracking. We assume limited in-hand object motion and demonstrations without repeated re-grasping or abrupt shifts in hand-object contact points. We also evaluate on an articulated object whose base remains stationary during demonstration and whose manipulated part is limited to one dominant axis of rotation.
3.2 Data Collection
The user first records a short RGB scan of the workspace without the manipulated objects, analogous to the environment scan used by UMI  [ 41 ] . The scan typically takes less than one minute. Structure-from-Motion (SfM) with LightGlue  [ 102 ] feature matching is then used to estimate camera poses and build a sparse 3D reconstruction of the environment. The resulting camera poses are used to initialize a 3D Gaussian Splat of the scene and to localize subsequent demonstrations.
The user then records task demonstrations in the workspace wearing a head-mounted egocentric camera. In our experiments, we use a GoPro Hero9 with a standard linear lens, modeled as a pinhole camera, although other monocular RGB cameras and mounting configurations could be used.
Figure 2: Overview of WARPED. Images of the scene are used to build a Gaussian Splat representation. The user then performs a tabletop manipulation task, recording an egocentric demonstration from a helmet-mounted GoPro. The demonstration is aligned with the Gaussian Splat, and the object pose is localized using vision foundation models. The object pose is then tracked via hand-object optimization. The resulting trajectories are retargeted to a robot gripper, and wrist-view renderings are synthesized and used to train a diffusion policy.
3.3 Interactive Scene Initialization
Interactive Scene Reconstruction.
For each demonstration, frames are localized within the static scene using Hierarchical Localization  [ 103 ] to estimate per-frame camera poses. SpatialTrackerV2  [ 104 ] is used to extract temporally consistent monocular depth maps, which are scale-aligned with the SfM reconstruction and Gaussian Splat. Additional details are provided in Appendix  B .
Hand Pose Initialization.
HAMER  [ 105 ] is used to obtain initial per-frame hand shape and pose estimates. The hand parameters are refined using a sequence-level optimization that enforces temporal smoothness and monocular depth consistency. A complete description can be found in Appendix  C .

Figure 6: Experimental setup: (a) helmet-mounted demonstration camera, (b) wrist-mounted rollout camera, (c) task objects, and (d) novel objects.
We evaluate WARPED on five rigid-object tabletop manipulation tasks and one articulated-object task using a UFactory xArm7 with an xArm G1 Gripper. For human demonstrations, a GoPro Hero9 is attached to a helmet (Fig.  6 (a)) worn by the user. For policy rollout, a GoPro Hero9 with Max Lens Mod 1.0 is mounted above the gripper to capture wrist-view observations (Fig.  6 (b)). For each task, we collect 30 demonstrations within a fixed workspace using a single object instance, and separately capture a single RGB image of the manipulated object for SAM3D mesh reconstruction. The demonstrations are then processed through WARPED and augmented 10 times (Sec.  3.6 ), and a diffusion policy is trained using four NVIDIA Tesla V100s.
Success rates are evaluated over 20 trials. Further details are provided in Appendix  H .
The evaluated tasks are shown in Fig.  7 . Object placement varies across trials. Rotate Box: The robot must rotate a box 90 degrees onto a new face. Pour Mug: The robot must grasp a mug by its handle and execute a pouring motion. Bottle from Dish Rack: The robot must pick up a bottle from the middle shelf of a dish rack. Wipe Plate with Brush: The robot must pick up a brush and wipe a plate. Can on Plate: The robot must pick up a can and place it onto an additionally localized plate. Open Microwave: The robot must pull the microwave door open by its handle.
Figure 7: Real-world rollouts for the evaluated tasks.
We compare WARPED against policies trained from teleoperated demonstrations collected with a Meta Quest 2 VR headset and controller. We additionally evaluate against the Alter baseline presented in RwoR  [ 43 ] .
The teleoperation and Alter baselines also use 30 demonstrations, but do not use the synthetic demonstration augmentations from Sec.  3.6 . Generating additional trajectories and observations from the original demonstrations is an advantage of WARPED’s synthetic data pipeline.
