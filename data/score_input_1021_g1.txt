In the following subsections, we detail our methodology by first formulating our state-conditioned Gaussian primitives and describing their integration within volume rendering ( Sec.   3.1 ).
We then introduce the parameterization of the differentiable slot-table ( Sec.   3.2 ), followed by our slot initialization procedure ( Sec.   3.3 ).
Finally, we describe our training pipeline and objectives that facilitate granular, state-controlled visualization during inference ( Secs.   3.4 and  3.5 ).
Terminology
A slot corresponds to one interactable object and holds two 3D representations for its binary states: “off” (e.g., door closed) and “on” (e.g., door open).
A slot activation is a per-frame binary variable indicating which state is currently observed.
All parts of the scene that remain constant throughout the capture are handled by the static base , which is shared across all configurations.
3.1. State-aware Gaussian Splat
Each 3D Gaussian primitive in our model is defined by its standard geometric and appearance attributes: mean μ \mu , covariance Σ \Sigma , opacity α \alpha , and view-dependent appearance  𝐜 \mathbf{c} .
To make our representation state-aware, we augment every Gaussian with a learnable categorical state distribution 𝐩 = softmax ⁡ ( ℓ ) \mathbf{p}=\operatorname{softmax}(\ell) over 1 + 2 ​ K 1+2K channels, where K K is the total number of object slots.
These channels comprise a single static base channel ℓ ⁡ [ 0 ] \ell[0] , together with a pair of off ( ℓ ⁡ [ 2 ​ k − 1 ] \ell[2k{-}1] ) and on ( ℓ ⁡ [ 2 ​ k ] \ell[2k] ) channels for each slot k ∈ { 1 , … , K } k\in\{1,\dots,K\} .
The two channels per slot — rather than one — let the softmax assign each Gaussian to a single configuration, so that disjoint subsets of primitives can independently model the geometry and appearance of an object in each of its two states (see  Figure   3 ).
This separation is essential because the on/off configurations of an object generally differ not just in appearance, but also in spatial extent — a closed door and an open door, for instance, occupy different parts of the scene — which a single shared primitive simply cannot represent.

Sparsity regularization To validate the effectiveness of our proposed algorithm in the absence of an existing dataset with variable object interactions, we collected a custom dataset using a consumer-grade Insta360 X4 dual-fisheye 360 ∘ 360^{\circ} camera.
This hardware choice reflects the typical setup of casual users.
During capture, we emulated non-expert behaviors, moving through the scene and interacting with objects—transitioning them from resting to active states and back.
Our dataset comprises 12 12 scenes organized into two groups.
The standard set contains 10 10 casually captured scenes ( 8 8 indoor and 2 2 outdoor), each featuring 3 3 – 9 9 object interactions.
The golden set contains 2 2 additional scenes for which we acquire controlled test sequences with novel camera trajectories and object-state combinations.
We preprocess the raw dual-fisheye data following FullCircle  ( Foroutan et al., 2026 ) to mask the capturer.
The calibration is performed in COLMAP using the masked fisheye frames, and then the fisheye images are undistorted into standard pinhole images.
For evaluation, we adhere to standard NeRF procedures, holding out every 8th frame as a test set.
Crucially , we exclude test samples that coincide with state transitions (i.e., frames where an object is actively being moved) to reduce noise in our metrics, though these transition frames are retained within the training set to measure whether models can learn robustly from casual capture data.
For the golden set sequences, we additionally evaluate novel camera frames, which show state configurations observed during training from new camera trajectories, and novel state-combination frames, which contain object-state configurations not observed during training.
This set is used to demonstrate our method’s ability to generalize beyond the training configurations, and provide true state-aware control.
4.1. Baselines
We evaluate our proposed method against several state-of-the-art scene representation baselines.
For static modeling, we compare against FullCircle   ( Foroutan et al., 2026 ) , utilizing its underlying 3DGUT   ( Wu et al., 2025a ) architecture as our primary reference.
To assess performance in dynamic contexts, we benchmark our framework against 4DGS   ( Yang et al., 2024b ) and DeGauss   ( Wang et al., 2025a ) .
For all quantitative and qualitative assessments, we utilize every 8th frame of the captured sequences as our standard evaluation set.

【结论/局限】
 We presented SwitchSplat, a 3D Gaussian Splatting framework that learns state-aware scene representations directly from casual video captures.
By decomposing the scene into a shared static base and a set of independently controllable slots, each modeling the binary “on”/“off” states of one interactable object, SwitchSplat decouples scene state from time – enabling a capability that time-conditioned dynamic methods fundamentally cannot provide: free composition of object configurations at inference, including combinations never observed during capture.
The slot table is initialized via a VLM and jointly refined end-to-end through differentiable rendering, requiring no manual annotation. and future work

The evaluation covers facial expression reconstruction, identity-specific body adaptation, and high-resolution detail recovery.
Data and protocol. Controlled face and body studies use chronological 80%/20% training–evaluation splits and fixed optimization budgets; checkpoint selection uses the training segment exclusively. Detail adaptation is evaluated on held-out frames, and cross-method rendering uses identical poses, cameras, backgrounds, and target-defined crops for every method. Scores are first aggregated per identity and then macro-averaged.
Metrics. Facial geometry is measured by Euclidean point error in the native shared tracked coordinates. Body adaptation reports refined RGB L1, boundary L1, and SSIM  [ 67 ] . Rendering comparisons use foreground PSNR  [ 68 ] together with SSIM and LPIPS  [ 82 ] inside a shared ground-truth person crop. Runtime measurements distinguish independently warmed stage throughput from measured resident-pipeline wall time. Semantic preservation is evaluated through fixed back-translation using BLEU-4  [ 39 ] and ROUGE-L F1  [ 31 ] on poses re-extracted from the rendered RGB videos.

References We presented Stable Signer V3, a sign language production framework combining shared facial primitives, test-time identity-specific body primitives, and a tunneling decoder for high-resolution appearance. Together, they unify expressive control and personalized rendering through a reusable deformable representation. Reference-video adaptation is performed once, after which the stored identity parameters drive subsequent signing sequences. On held-out evaluations, the three modules reduce head reconstruction error by 71.4%, body-boundary error by 35.1% across ten identities, and high-resolution RGB reconstruction error by 24.2%, while retaining interactive inference. Future work will broaden signer and motion coverage and include evaluation by proficient sign-language users.

III-F Discretization-Agnostic Animation Binding III-A Framework Overview and Problem Formulation
PhysSkin++ is a generalizable physics-informed framework for real-time physics-based animation across diverse 3D shapes and discretizations. As illustrated in Fig.  2 , our neural skinning fields autoencoder predicts continuous skinning fields from static geometry to define a deformation subspace mapping. The autoencoder is trained through Physics-Informed Self-Supervised Learning (PISSL) under physical and numerical constraints. At runtime, we advance reduced coordinates to animate objects with boundary conditions, interactive control, and contact. Beyond purely physics-driven animation, PhysSkin++ also supports rig-complementary animation by learning complementary deformation subspaces for prescribed rigs. Moreover, the continuous deformation map supports binding to mesh vertices, sampled points, and 3D Gaussians.
Fig. 2: Given a static 3D shape, we first sample volumetric cubature points for animation and surface points for shape encoding. A shape encoder processes the surface points to produce shape latents, from which a set of learnable queries extract handle latents via cross-attention. Subsequently, spatial queries attend to the handle latents through another cross-attention block to derive point-wise features, which are decoded into neural skinning fields. A geometry-only, physics-informed learning strategy optimizes the network in a self-supervised manner under physical and orthogonality constraints, enabling the learned skinning fields to support real-time, physics-based animation.
Classical full-space physics-based animation methods  [ 51 , 38 , 27 , 61 , 31 , 63 ] typically use explicit or implicit time integrators to update the object’s motion state. Explicit time integrators are often constrained by stability conditions  [ 33 ] , while implicit time integrators require solving a large nonlinear optimization problem in the high-dimensional full space. PhysSkin++ instead represents the deformation using continuous neural skinning fields and advances only the corresponding reduced-order subspace coordinates through implicit time integration. This lower-dimensional optimization reduces computational cost  [ 6 ] , enabling real-time physics-based animation.

We present PhysSkin++ , a generalizable physics-informed framework for real-time physics-based animation across diverse 3D shapes and discretizations by learning neural skinning fields directly from static geometries. Its Neural Skinning Fields Autoencoder employs a transformer-based shape encoder and a cross-attention decoder to predict continuous skinning fields.
•
We develop a Physics-Informed Self-Supervised Learning (PISSL) strategy that incorporates on-the-fly skinning-field normalization and conflict-aware gradient correction, enabling stable joint optimization of multiple self-supervised loss terms.

We develop a controllable and collision-aware reduced dynamics formulation that supports Dirichlet boundary conditions, interactive control, environmental interaction, and multi-object contact, together with discretization-agnostic binding of the continuous deformation map to meshes, point clouds, and 3D Gaussian representations.

7.3 Toward User-Led MR Adaptation and Beyond & Conclusion
We set out to understand whether users can control the adaptive behavior of MR interfaces through natural language, and what such control offers over direct manipulation. Participants expressed a broad range of adaptation needs in speech, and used ConverseMR for most of their adaptations whenever it was available. ConverseMR alone, however, was never rated highest on any measure. It was statistically indistinguishable from DM on seven of eight usability items and worse on the time spent correcting mistakes, while the combination of both methods was preferred throughout. We discuss below what this pattern means for the design of conversational adaptation, and for the role users can take in adapting MR interfaces.

These findings were unexpected, as we anticipated that the results would align with the broader pattern observed in the literature:
explicit tracking rewards [ 22 , 28 , 8 ] consistently produce higher single-motion fidelity than adversarial approaches [ 25 ] , a trade-off also highlighted by Li et al. [ 15 ] .
Our results suggest that, while the two are not equivalent, the difference for ADD is relatively small.
Adversarial methods ( AMP, ADD ) offer practical advantages—no per-component reward tuning, implicit style capture, more controller flexibility—but at a measurable fidelity cost. ADD narrows this gap substantially compared to AMP , but DeepMimic remains uniformly superior; for production scenarios requiring faithful reproduction of specific motions, the use of explicit rewards like in DeepMimic remains the most reliable approach. However, when several animations need to be combined more flexibly, ADD seems to provide a viable option.
In ADD [ 37 ] , the discriminator saturates rapidly (reaching near-100% accuracy within 20M steps, since the zero-displacement positive sample is trivially separable), after which the style reward gradient vanishes. We emphasize that this saturation is a fundamental architectural limitation of the displacement-based formulation, not a tuning problem: the discriminator’s positive class is a single point (the zero vector), which is trivially separable from any non-zero displacement distribution regardless of network capacity, regularization, or training schedule. A small hybrid task reward component (5–15% weight) resolves this by maintaining gradient flow after discriminator saturation.
With this fix, all 16 motions achieved 100% survival in Unity3D and WebXR deployment.

2.3.1 Training strategy
To find common ground for both pipelines, we chose to do all training with simulation steps every 0.008 s (125Hz), and up to 100M simulation steps. This took approximately 40 hours for each training run based on modular-agents , and 40 minutes for each training run based on playground .
All playground training runs were performed on an Intel Core i9-14900K, 64 GB of RAM, and two NVIDIA GeForce RTX 4090 graphics cards. All modular-agents training runs were done either on a Windows 11 Pro with an Intel Core i9-14900K, 64 GB of RAM, and a NVIDIA RTX 4070 Ti Super graphics card, or a Windows 11 Pro with an AMD Ryzen Threadripper 3970X and 128 GB of RAM. The configuration files, including the environment and PPO hyperparameters, can be found in the supplementary materials.
2.3.2 How to compare different controller policies in VR
Practical validation of the method included compilation for different platforms (Windows, Linux, MacOS, Android) to confirm the models could be used in production projects.
Since Android builds are not included in MuJoCo releases, we recompiled the physics engine from its source to integrate it into VR builds. We also compared these builds with running the entire environment in a web browser.
To compare the movement quality, independently of the controller policy used and the delivery method (Unity3D, WebXR), we recorded the movement data generated by the controller for exactly one animation loop, with no perturbations. We analysed the data with a set of custom Python scripts (which we provide in the supplementary materials). This allowed us to compare different training pipelines and controllers in the same context.

4.2 Building Blocks
4.2.1 B1: Splat Renderer
Developer Workflow and Developer Experience
SplatXR is implemented as a Unity package, thus making it easily installable through Unity’s Package Manager.
It features a developer-facing graphical interface, integrated into the Unity Editor, that provides access to a set of well-defined building blocks through panels, scripts, materials, and shaders.
Building blocks can be implemented as needed, and previewed interactively in the Unity Editor scene view.
Notably, this makes SplatXR ’s splat renderer compatible with non-XR applications as well.
4.2. Building Blocks
SplatXR comprises four main building blocks:
B1:
a Splat Renderer building block enabling splat rendering on XR headsets in passthrough AR or full VR with computational constraints
B2:
a Splat Physics building block based on our mesh augmentation procedures, enabling both shadows, cast from splats onto the environment, and rigid-body collisions between splats, classical meshes, and the environment
B3:
a Splat Effects building block based on customizable shader programs, enabling compelling visual experiences such as object dis- and re-integration
B4:
a Splat Blending building block enabling blending between the physical scene and the virtual scene in AR
Building blocks can be accessed by developers via the developer interface , which is implemented as a dedicated panel in the Unity Editor. Actions performed in the developer interface add or modify assets in the project folder as well as game objects and attached components in the scene hierarchy. We show our editor interface in more detail in Appendix A.1 .
4.2.1. B1: Splat Renderer
The Splat Renderer building block comprises a 3D Gaussian splat rendering pipeline designed for compatibility with standalone XR devices, including Meta Quest and Apple Vision Pro.
On both Quest and Vision Pro, it can render fully immersive splat-based VR scenes, and on Quest, it can render splats and blend them with the passthrough layer for situated AR experiences. 2 2

5.4 Hypotheses
5.5 Measures For the experiment, we evaluated the three avatar head-gaze interaction methods introduced in Section  4.3 (VR, Head, Touch) across the two fault detection tasks described above (T1: Same Machine and T2: Twin Machine).
To control puzzle behavior, we defined two difficulty levels based on the number of incorrect stickers that had to be inspected before the solution became available: at level 1, the first inspected sticker was always incorrect and the next one was the solution; at level 2, the first two inspected stickers were incorrect and the solution appeared only on a later sticker. The system tracked inspected stickers and dynamically set the solution position accordingly.
We used a within-subjects design in which all participants, acting as remote experts, experienced all interaction methods, both tasks, and both difficulty levels. Conditions were counterbalanced using a Latin square distribution to reduce order effects. In total, each participant completed 2 2  tasks × \times 3 3  interaction methods × \times 2 2  difficulty levels × \times 3 3  repetitions, resulting in 36 36  trials per participant. Because each condition combines a specific visualization, display, and control method, the study compares bundled interface configurations rather than isolated factors; causal attribution to individual dimensions is not possible.
5.4. Hypotheses
We formulated our hypotheses to compare the 3 avatar’s gaze control methods in terms of task performance, perceived workload, and user preference, as well as the overall effectiveness of the tabletop avatar control for remote collaboration.
H1 (T1 – Same Machine). For T1, which focuses on detailed inspection around a single machine, we expect the VR condition to outperform Head and Touch. Alignment between interaction and visualization spaces has been shown to reduce mental effort and improve performance  ( Grasset et al., 2005 ) , and our pilot (Section  4.3 ) indicated that direct head control in an immersive view felt most natural for precise targeting. We therefore hypothesize that VR will yield faster task completion, lower perceived workload, and higher user preference.
H2 (T2 – Twin Machine). For T2, which requires parallel attention to two spatially separated machines, we expect Head and Touch to outperform VR. As discussed in Section  3.2 , full immersion temporarily removes direct access to the exocentric tabletop overview needed for cross-machine coordination. Because Head and Touch preserve continuous tabletop visibility while T2 demands monitoring both machines, we hypothesize they will yield faster completion, lower workload, and higher preference than VR.

6.2 Application prototypes The evaluation confirmed the validity of our sensing principle and our implementation on a wrist-worn device to capture body-coupled events.
It also confirmed the TapID configuration of our prototype, embedding two low-cost, low-power IMUs in the silicone strap.
Regarding the tap detection, the accuracy TapID achieved is high enough for practical purposes, but it also is in Fitbit and Smartwatch configuration.
This confirms recent results in related efforts (e.g.,  [ 17 ] ), though with IMU sensors positioned at the wrist for unencumbered hands instead of placing them on the fingers.
One trade-off remains in our tap detection: Decreasing the back-off period allows for faster interactions but increases the likelihood that one (strong) tap produces two events (i.e., one false positive).
As for the performance of our classifier to identify individual fingers, unsurprisingly we saw the best cross-person accuracy when training with the largest amount possible, refined with person-specific training data, reaching F 1 F_{1} scores of up to .96 this way (Figure  9 ).
