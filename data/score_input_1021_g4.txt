3.5.2. Stage II: Geometric Stabilization
Optimizing solely on a single trigger viewpoint P atk P_{\text{atk}} constitutes an ill-posed problem in 3D reconstruction. Without spatial consistency constraints, the optimization lacks sufficient geometric supervision, leading to a rank-deficient solution space that often collapses into degenerate, “billboard-like” artifacts (e.g., floating black clouds). As visualized in Figure  4 (left), such artifacts severely violate the stealthiness condition in Eq. ( 3 ) even under minor camera deviations from the trigger pose. To address this, we propose Viewpoint Ensemble Stabilization (VES) , a geometric regularization strategy that embeds the backdoor coherently within the local 3D structure—rather than painting it onto a single 2D projection—by enforcing rendering consistency across a local viewpoint manifold.

4.1.1. Datasets
We use Blender ( Mildenhall et al., 2021 ) and Mip-NeRF360 ( Barron et al., 2022 ) , which are standard datasets in NeRF ( Mildenhall et al., 2021 ) and 3DGS ( Kerbl et al., 2023 ) research.
The Blender dataset contains eight objects, each consisting of 400 400 images captured from different viewpoints on the upper hemisphere, with a resolution of 800 × 800 800\times 800 pixels. The Mip-NeRF360 dataset comprises nine scenes, including five outdoor scenes and four indoor scenes, each featuring a complex central object or region with intricate and highly detailed backgrounds. on Synthetic Dataset
In this section, we evaluate the attack performance of GaussTrap on the Blender dataset ( Mildenhall et al., 2021 ) . We select two challenging attack images, "Earth" and "Starry", which exhibit low visual similarity to the target scenes, making them suitable for assessing the generalization capability of the attack.
As shown in Table  1 , GaussTrap consistently outperforms all baseline methods across all four evaluation viewpoints, demonstrating superior robustness and generalization. The results are averaged over 10 10 runs for statistical reliability. The visualization in Figure  6 further highlights the high visual fidelity achieved by our method, providing qualitative evidence that complements the quantitative metrics. Together, these results show that GaussTrap effectively injects high-quality adversarial content at the target viewpoint while preserving visual fidelity at other viewpoints. By leveraging the properties of the 3DGS framework, it strikes a strong balance between attack effectiveness and stealthiness, making the attack both powerful and difficult to detect across different viewpoints. on real-world dataset
To further evaluate the generality and practicality of our approach across diverse 3D reconstruction scenarios, we conduct comprehensive experiments on the Mip-NeRF360 dataset. Motivated by real-world deployment scenarios of 3D models, we design attack images that remain semantically consistent with the original scenes while intentionally introducing misleading objects, such as speed limit signs, pedestrians, and vehicles, to simulate plausible adversarial perturbations. The stabilization angle is set to 5 ∘ 5^{\circ} .

Dataset
We use the AMASS  [ 43 ] dataset for training and simulated evaluation. Sequences are resampled to 30fps and preprocessed following HuMoR  [ 51 ] , adjusting the floor and annotating foot contact labels. We train on the AMASS train split with stochastic augmentation (Sec.  3.4 ).
We evaluate on AMASS validation and test splits. The validation split is included specifically to enable long-sequence evaluation, as the standard test split alone is relatively short.
Training Details
We train using AdamW optimizer with a learning rate 10 − 4 10^{-4} , weight decay 10 − 4 10^{-4} , and a batch size of 32 for 16 hours on 8 A5000 GPUs. Max training sequence length is 512 with attention horizon W = ± 63 W=\pm 63 , implemented via FlexAttention  [ 17 ] .
Test-time optimization uses Theseus  [ 48 ] Levenberg-Marquardt optimizer.
Additional details are provided in the supplementary material.
Metrics
We use Mean Per Joint Position Error ( MPJPE , mm), Mean Per Joint Velocity Error ( MPJVE , cm/s), and Jerk (km/s 3 ) (the third derivative of position) to evaluate motion quality.
To assess hand observations 𝐇 \mathbf{H} , we use Hand Position Error ( Hand PE , mm) and Visible Hand Position Error ( Vis Hand PE , mm, Hand PE on visible frames).
For shape, we use Height Error (cm) and Span Error (cm) for accuracy and Height Standard Deviation ( Height Std , cm) and Span Standard Deviation ( Span Std , cm) for consistency. Height and Span are defined as the maximum vertical and horizontal vertex distances in a T-pose.

While our feed-forward Vis Hand PE is slightly higher than EgoAllo’s optimized version, our overall Hand PE is significantly lower. This indicates that our approach robustly localizes hands even during prolonged occlusions. EgoAllo relies on a head-only prior that, due to its inherent ambiguity, frequently hallucinates invisible hands back into the FoV (Fig.  4 ). Because its test-time optimization cannot fully correct this error, it causes arms to unnaturally snap to observed positions upon re-entry. By conditioning on hand cues and the visibility-encoding global context 𝓖 \bm{\mathcal{G}} , our prior avoids this issue and accurately infers the position of unseen hands outside the FoV.

Setup.
We evaluate on DL3DV  [ 20 ] (140 test scenes) and RealEstate10K  [ 50 ] (1,600 test scenes).
On DL3DV, context views ( N ∈ { 6 , 12 , 24 } N\in\{6,12,24\} ) are selected via farthest point sampling with maximum frame gaps of 50, 100, and 150; on RE10K, we use N = 6 N{=}6 .
In both cases, 8 target views are held out for evaluation.
Each method encodes at its native training resolution, and we render and evaluate all renderings at a common 252 × 252 252{\times}252 resolution ( Sec.   0.B.1 ).
Following standard practice  [ 41 , 14 ] , predicted Gaussians are aligned to ground-truth target views before computing PSNR, SSIM, and LPIPS.
Baselines.
We benchmark against pose-free methods AnySplat  [ 14 ] , C3G  [ 1 ] , and YoNoSplat  [ 40 ] , as well as NoPoSplat  [ 41 ] , which requires ground-truth intrinsics.
Since ZipSplat optionally accepts camera priors, we additionally compare to the posed MVSplat  [ 6 ] and DepthSplat  [ 39 ] .
We further include DA3  [ 19 ] , a pixel-aligned Gaussian head on the same backbone as ZipSplat, isolating the contribution of our token-based decoder.

Token test-time optimization.
When ground-truth poses are available, ZipSplat can be improved further at test time by freezing the decoder and optimizing the scene tokens, at a fixed Gaussian budget.
This is fast and effective: 50 steps ( ∼ \sim 3 s on a single 4090) add about 5 5  dB PSNR, and full convergence reaches 5.8 5.8  dB while halving LPIPS at 24 views ( Tab.   1 ).
That adjusting the tokens alone recovers this much quality shows the token representation has substantial headroom, and that improving the feed-forward prediction is a promising direction.

【结论/局限】
0.A Additional Implementation Details We present ZipSplat, a feed-forward 3D Gaussian Splatting architecture that decouples primitive placement from the 2D pixel grid by predicting unconstrained Gaussians from a compact set of scene tokens.
This token-based formulation achieves state-of-the-art, pose-free novel view synthesis while using up to 6 × 6{\times} fewer Gaussians than pixel-aligned baselines and offering continuous inference-time control over the Gaussian budget.
By allocating capacity based on 3D geometric complexity rather than camera resolution, ZipSplat produces compact and scalable scene representations from sparse multi-view input.
This efficiency opens two paths for future work.
First, ZipSplat’s compact representation benefits semantic scene understanding, where per-Gaussian features quickly dominate memory.
Second, decoupling primitives from the spatial grid offers a direct extension to 4D dynamic scenes, where token clustering can compress redundant observations across both space and time.
More broadly, we see ZipSplat as a step toward feed-forward reconstruction that predicts geometry directly in 3D, rather than uplifting 2D predictions along viewing rays.

Following LYRA  [ 1 ] , we represent the scene as 3D Gaussian primitives 𝒢 \mathcal{G} , which support efficient differentiable rendering at arbitrary camera poses.
The Plücker ray embeddings 𝐑 \mathbf{R} , computed from the target camera poses { c t v } \{c^{v}_{t}\} , encode the geometric relationship between each target viewpoint and the scene.

References In this work, we present PRISM, a feed-forward framework for single-image 3D reconstruction that shows diffusion sampling supervises training but is not needed at inference to synthesize competitive novel views.
PRISM separates what geometry can already explain from what remains uncertain, which allows a lightweight feed-forward encoder to replace iterative diffusion sampling at inference.
The two-stage training strategy transfers a model trained without real-world supervision to real scenes: latents supervised distillation aligns the encoder with the frozen 3DGS decoder, and perceptual fine-tuning closes the appearance gap.
PRISM matches geometry-based reconstruction in efficiency while staying competitive in fidelity.

C. Communication Analysis
We next analyze the size and number of perception messages exchanged in our real-world datasets using simulated wireless communication.
We summarize the size of perception messages for the proposed method in Tab.  IV .
The “Point-cloud Raw” baseline we compared against refers to a brute-force centralized strategy, where all raw point clouds are transmitted to a centralized server.
To further reduce the communication overhead of our framework, only limited object information is shared, which includes the object ID, centroid position, dimensions and semantic label.
Object data is only exchanged when there is a significant update to the scene graph, such as when a new object is added or a large odometry drift is corrected.
This strategy enables us to publish the object data at a much lower frequency compared to the LiDAR sensor frequency.
Once a relative graph transformation estimation is computed, the relevant keyframes are requested from the robot neighbors.
A keyframe is only sent after the requesting robot confirms it has not been sent previously.
The point cloud keyframe message is compressed using PCL  [ 34 ] to minimize its size.
During the experiments, we observed that keyframe requests were sent at a much higher frequency than the keyframe responses.
This occurred mainly because, once two scene graphs are matched, a burst of similar requests are sent to the neighbor, causing the oldest requests to be dropped.
Further optimization, such as implementing a delayed request strategy, may be needed to improve performance further.

VI Conclusion
In this paper, we propose SGM-SLAM, a data-efficient distributed SLAM method using limited object data scene graph matching.
We extract geometric and semantic information from LiDAR and camera data, and construct a multi-level scene graph collaboratively with the multi-robot team.
The introduction of object-level graph matching improves the generality, robustness, and communication efficiency of our distributed SLAM system in both indoor and outdoor environments.
Future work will evaluate the framework under explicitly bandwidth-limited and intermittent communication conditions and investigate deployment in larger-scale field environments.

Dataset
We conduct our evaluation across three public datasets for a comprehensive experiment. Mip-NeRF 360   [ BMV∗22 ]
contains four bounded indoor rooms with strong specular clutter and five unbounded outdoor scenes with foliage occlusion and high dynamic range lighting.
We follow the 3DGS protocol  [ KKLD23 ] :
every 8 th view is held out for testing.
We also experiment on Light-Field (LF) dataset [ YSHWSH16 ] , which offers four tabletop objects torch , statue , basket ,
and africa captured by a motorised gantry.
A salient trait is the foreground-centric framing : each
target object remains centred across all views, yielding
stable masks from SAM-2 and making the dataset ideal for
evaluating per-object calibration.
In addition we target the train and truck from Tanks & Temples (TNT)   [ KPZK17 ] , which offers large-scale, drone-style outdoor captures characterised by long-baseline parallax and strong depth discontinuities.
Inspired by the sparse-view protocol of Shen  [ SAMNR22 ] , we additionally include intentionally biased and imbalanced camera orbits. They exacerbate parallax/occlusion artifacts and thus form a stress test for Fisher-based uncertainty modeling.
All RGB frames are kept at full original resolution to preserve fine geometric cues; no cropping or scaling is applied.
